Search for sleep sounds and you'll run into the colors almost immediately: white noise, pink noise, brown noise, sometimes green or blue as well. Each comes with somebody insisting it's the one that finally worked. It's a lot less mysterious than it sounds, and once you know what the colors mean, picking one gets much easier.

## What the colors actually mean

All three are the same idea: a steady, random hiss with no rhythm or melody. The only difference is how the sound's energy is spread between low and high pitches.

- **White noise** has equal energy at every frequency. To our ears that sounds bright and hissy, because hearing is more sensitive to the higher pitches, and there are a lot more of them. Think of an untuned radio or TV static.
- **Pink noise** turns the high end down gradually, so each octave carries the same energy. It sounds softer and fuller than white noise, closer to steady rain or wind through leaves.
- **Brown noise** (named after Robert Brown, of Brownian motion, not the color) turns the high end down even more steeply. It's deep and rumbly: distant surf, a strong wind, the inside of an airplane cabin.

So "pink" and "brown" aren't different kinds of sound. They're white noise with progressively more of the treble taken away.

## Why any of them help you sleep

The main thing a steady sound does is **masking**. What wakes you up usually isn't loud noise. It's change: a car door, a dog, a partner's snore, the furnace clicking on. Your brain keeps half an ear on the world while you sleep and flags anything new.

A constant sound raises the background level, so those sudden changes stand out less against it. The car door is still there; it just isn't a spike out of silence anymore. That's also why the sound needs to be steady. Anything with a rhythm, a melody or a noticeable loop point gives your brain something new to notice, which is the opposite of the point.

## What the research shows (and doesn't)

The honest answer is that the evidence is thinner than the internet suggests.

- **Masking is well supported** as an idea, and plenty of people find a steady sound helps them fall asleep and stay asleep, particularly in noisy places.
- **Pink noise has the most interesting research**, but much of it is small studies, and some of the best-known ones played short bursts of pink noise timed to the sleeper's own brain waves. In [one such study of older adults](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00109), those timed bursts deepened slow-wave sleep. That isn't the same as leaving a sound on all night.
- **[A 2021 systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2020.101385)** that pulled together the studies on continuous noise as a sleep aid rated the quality of evidence as very low. Not that it doesn't work, just that it hasn't been well studied, and nobody has proven one color beats another for everyone. The authors also point out that loud noise all night can itself disturb sleep and affect hearing, which is one more reason to keep the volume down.

In other words: there's no strong evidence that you need a particular color. There's good reason to think the best one is the one *you* find easiest to ignore.

## How to choose

1. **If high, hissy sounds annoy you**, skip white noise and try something deeper. Many people who "don't like white noise" turn out to like brown noise a lot.
2. **If you're masking voices or TV through a wall**, a fuller sound with more midrange (pink-ish, like steady rain) usually covers speech better than a very deep one.
3. **If you're masking low rumbles**, like traffic or a neighbor's bass, a deeper sound (brown-ish, like ocean surf) matches them better.
4. **Give it a few nights.** Any new sound is noticeable at first, which is exactly what you don't want at bedtime. It takes a little while to fade into the background.

## Where natural sounds fit in

Natural sounds have colors too, roughly speaking. Steady rain and a babbling stream sit close to pink noise. Ocean waves, distant surf and strong wind lean toward brown. A fan or a clothes dryer is a broad, even whoosh. That's why so many people sleep well to them: they do the same masking job, with a texture that feels less clinical than pure static.

- **Keep the volume low.** It only needs to be loud enough to soften sudden noises, not to drown everything out. Around the level of a quiet conversation is plenty for most rooms, and quieter is fine.
- **Put the phone or speaker across the room**, not on your pillow.
